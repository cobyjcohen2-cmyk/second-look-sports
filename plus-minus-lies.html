The Basketball Stat Everyone Misreads: Why Plus/Minus Lies More Than It Tells You

Basketball · Analytics · 8 min read

Turn on any NBA broadcast and eventually someone will point at a plus/minus number as proof of a player's impact. "He's a +14 tonight, look how much better the team is with him out there." It sounds like hard evidence. It's usually closer to noise wearing a stat's clothing.

Plus/minus, in its simplest form, measures the point differential while a given player is on the floor. It's appealing because it's holistic — unlike points or rebounds, it doesn't care what a player did, only what happened to the scoreboard while they were present. That's also exactly why it's misleading.

The problem is who you share the court with

A player's raw plus/minus is inseparable from the four teammates around him and the five opponents in front of him. Put a mediocre defender next to four All-Stars, and his plus/minus will look outstanding — not because of anything he's doing, but because he's rarely on the floor without elite talent absorbing the difficult possessions. Flip it around: a genuinely good role player who spends most of his minutes anchoring the second unit against other teams' starters will post a mediocre or negative number despite outplaying his role every night.

This is why bench specialists — the ones who enter games to steady a unit of backups — are consistently punished by raw plus/minus, while players who share the floor with a superstar for 30 minutes a night are consistently flattered by it.

Plus/minus doesn't measure a player. It measures a lineup, and then assigns the result to one name on it.

Sample size makes it worse

A single game's plus/minus is close to meaningless — a few lucky bounces, a couple of opponent misses, and the number swings by ten or more with no change in actual play quality. Even over a full season, raw plus/minus for a role player who logs 18 minutes a night is built on a relatively small number of "possessions where it was just him and four specific teammates against five specific opponents." The noise doesn't fully wash out until you're looking at multiple seasons of data, which is longer than most conversations about the stat ever wait.

What actually filters the noise out

The analytics community moved past raw plus/minus for a reason. Adjusted plus/minus (APM) and its regularized cousin RAPM (regularized adjusted plus/minus) try to isolate a player's individual contribution by using the full mix of lineups a player appears in across a season, statistically controlling for teammates and opponents. It's not perfect — it still needs large samples to stabilize, and it can undervalue players in low-usage, high-efficiency roles — but it answers the actual question raw plus/minus pretends to answer: what does this specific player add, independent of who's standing next to him?

A simpler gut-check, if you don't want to dig into APM databases: look at on/off splits combined with usage rate and role. A player with a strong on/off number who also carries real offensive responsibility (high usage, not just spotting up in the corner) is a much safer signal than plus/minus alone. A player who looks great only because his on-court minutes overlap almost entirely with a superstar's is the pattern to be suspicious of.

The takeaway

Plus/minus isn't useless — a player who posts strong on/off numbers across multiple seasons, multiple teams, and multiple lineup combinations is very likely a genuinely good two-way contributor. The mistake is treating a single season, or worse, a single game, as proof of anything. The next time a broadcast leans on a plus/minus number to make a point, the more interesting question is who else was on the floor.
