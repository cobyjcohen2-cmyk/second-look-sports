The Most Undervalued Position in Fantasy Drafts (And Why Everyone Overpays for RBs)

Football · Fantasy Strategy · 9 min read

Every fantasy draft follows the same script. The first ten picks are running backs. Half the room justifies it with some version of "you can't win without one," and by round three, the running back position has been drained while wide receivers and even a tight end or two of similar season-long value are still sitting on the board. This isn't strategy. It's habit dressed up as strategy.

Value isn't about how good a player is — it's about what you'd lose without them

The concept fantasy drafters consistently skip is replacement value: a player's worth isn't just what they score, it's the gap between what they score and what you could get for free off the waiver wire at the same position. A running back who scores 14 points a week sounds solid until you realize the RB2 on your bench, or a streaming option available in free agency, scores 10. That's a 4-point edge. Meanwhile, a wide receiver scoring 14 points a week might be sitting in a position where the replacement-level free agent option only scores 6 — an 8-point edge, double the running back's actual marginal value, despite an identical raw score.

This is the entire argument for "positional scarcity," and it's why annual college-level fantasy analysis keeps landing on the same conclusion: running back as a position is deep. Not every individual running back — the true bell-cow, 20-touch-a-game workhorse is genuinely rare — but the dropoff from RB1 to a usable streaming replacement is much shallower than drafters behave like it is. Meanwhile at wide receiver and, especially, at tight end, the gap between a startable option and replacement level is often wider than it looks on a point-per-game leaderboard.

The question isn't "who scores the most." It's "who costs me the most to lose."

Why tight end is the position drafters ignore hardest

Tight end is the clearest version of this mistake. Most leagues have a small handful of tight ends who consistently outscore the position's replacement level by a wide margin, and a massive cliff after that — where the twelfth-best tight end available on waivers might score half of what the top options do. That's a bigger structural edge than almost anything available at running back by the third round, and yet tight end routinely goes undrafted until double-digit rounds because "he's just a tight end" feels true even when the math says otherwise.

The running back injury tax

There's a second reason the RB-heavy strategy underperforms its reputation: running backs get hurt more, and more severely, than any other offensive skill position, largely because of workload and contact volume. Spending three of your first four picks on a position with an elevated injury rate isn't just a value question — it's a risk-concentration question. A roster built early around two or three running backs is a roster where one bad Sunday can erase a third of your starting lineup's floor.

What this means on draft day

None of this means "never draft a running back early" — true workhorse backs with a locked-in role are still some of the highest-value picks in the game, because their replacement-level dropoff really is steep. The mistake is treating the whole position that way by habit, when the actual math should be evaluated player by player and league by league, using replacement value rather than raw projected points. The drafters who consistently finish near the top of standings aren't the ones following the "running backs early" script — they're the ones who ran the replacement-value math on every position before the draft even started.
